On September 29, the Supreme Court stayed the First Circuit's judgment in *D.V.D. v. Department of Homeland Security*, treated the government's stay application as a petition for certiorari, and granted it the same day. The case is now *Department of Homeland Security v. D.V.D.*, No. 26-426, and it will be argued December 9.

The stay is easy to misread. It did not stop third country removals. It paused the judgment that had blocked them, so **DHS may carry out third country removals under its 2025 guidance while the case is pending.** The only people protected in the meantime are those with their own court orders.

I built the [bond split map](/blog/immigration-bond-circuit-split-map-and-data) because eleven circuits had answered the same question. This case is different. The third country question never produced a split on the merits, and the reason why is the most interesting thing about it. This page shows what the courts have done anyway, with links to every opinion and order.

## The question

When an immigration judge orders someone removed but grants withholding of removal or protection under the Convention Against Torture as to their home country, DHS may still send that person somewhere else. In March 2025, and again in July 2025, DHS issued guidance on how. Where a receiving country gives diplomatic assurances, the guidance allows removal with no notice at all. Otherwise it allows removal 24 hours after notice, or as little as 6 hours in undefined "exigent circumstances," with a fear screening only if the person raises a fear on their own.

A nationwide class challenged that guidance in the District of Massachusetts. The district court set it aside, and on September 18, 2026 the First Circuit largely affirmed, holding that the statutes and regulations require meaningful notice and a meaningful opportunity to raise a fear of persecution or torture before removal to a country not named in the removal order.

The Supreme Court directed the parties to brief four questions: whether the district court had jurisdiction; whether 8 U.S.C. 1252(f)(1) allowed classwide declaratory relief and APA vacatur; whether the guidance is unlawful under 8 U.S.C. 1231(b), the Due Process Clause, or FARRA and the CAT regulations; and any other question the government thinks necessary.

## Where things stand right now

Court orders have switched the guidance on and off six times since April 2025. For most of that time, it has been in effect.

| Period | Status of the guidance | Why |
|---|---|---|
| April 18 to June 23, 2025 | Limited by court order | The District of Massachusetts required written notice and a chance to raise fear claims before any third country removal |
| June 23, 2025 to February 25, 2026 | In effect | The Supreme Court stayed that injunction without explanation |
| February 25 to March 16, 2026 | Set aside | The district court entered final judgment vacating the guidance |
| March 16 to September 23, 2026 | In effect | A divided First Circuit panel stayed the judgment pending appeal |
| September 23 to 29, 2026 | Set aside | The First Circuit affirmed and dissolved its stay |
| Since September 29, 2026 | In effect | The Supreme Court stayed the judgment and granted certiorari |

Three things follow. First, DHS can use the full guidance today, including removal with no notice at all to a country that has given diplomatic assurances, and removal 24 hours after notice, or 6 hours in exigent circumstances, everywhere else. Second, the stay is not a ruling on the merits; the Court will decide whether the guidance is lawful after the December 9 argument, most likely by the end of the Term next summer. Third, the Supreme Court's order reaches only the classwide judgment. A district court order protecting a specific person, like those in *Ansumana S.*, *Cruz-Medina*, or the Fourth Circuit's stay in *Bojorquez*, still binds the government for that person. That is why individual habeas petitions remain the only way to stop a particular removal while the case is pending.

## Two roads into court

There are two ways to challenge an immigration detention or removal policy in federal court, and the choice of road decides what kind of law gets made.

| | The bond question | The third country question |
|---|---|---|
| How it was litigated | Individual habeas petitions, each filed where the detainee was held | One nationwide class action under the APA, alongside thousands of individual habeas petitions |
| Why that road | Habeas must be filed against the immediate custodian in the district of confinement, and *Garland v. Aleman Gonzalez* bars classwide injunctions against the detention statutes | The class asked for declaratory relief and vacatur of the guidance, which the plaintiffs argue § 1252(f)(1) does not reach |
| Courts of appeals heard from | Eleven | One, with an appeal pending in a second |
| What reached the Supreme Court | A circuit split | A single classwide judgment |

The bond question reached eleven circuits because each detainee had to sue where he was held. Under *Rumsfeld v. Padilla*, a habeas petition challenging physical custody goes against the immediate custodian in the district of confinement, and in *Trump v. J.G.G.* the Court sent the Alien Enemies Act detainees down that same road. Because ICE holds people all over the country, district judges in nearly every state decided the same statutory question at once, and their rulings flowed up to nearly every court of appeals.

The third country question took the other road. The *D.V.D.* plaintiffs challenged DHS guidance as agency action under the APA, on behalf of a nationwide class that does not allow opting out, and asked for declaratory relief and vacatur instead of an injunction. One district court could then decide the policy for everyone. No other court of appeals ever had the policy in front of it, which is why the merits side of the map is nearly empty.

### The class as a shield

The class did not stop individual cases. It changed what the government argues in them. In habeas cases from Texas and Colorado, the government's filings say the petitioner is a member of the *D.V.D.* class, is bound by it, and cannot litigate the same procedures separately. After the Supreme Court stayed the class injunction in June 2025 without explanation, that argument meant a class member had no protection from the class and, on the government's view, no right to seek it alone.

District courts have mostly refused to accept that. *Cruz-Medina v. Noem* (D. Md. 2025) declined to read the Supreme Court's unexplained stay as a ruling on the merits, agreeing with a Washington court that the government's main argument in *D.V.D.*, about classwide relief, would have no bearing on an individual petition. *E.D.Q.C. v. Warden* (M.D. Ga. 2025) refused to pause a case for a man sent to El Salvador while *D.V.D.* played out. *Ansumana S. v. Blanche* (D. Minn. 2026) held that class membership neither defeated jurisdiction nor justified declining it, and quoted *Kumar v. Wamsley* (W.D. Wash. 2025) for the practical point: if the petitioner waits for the class, he could be removed in the meantime.

The individual cases also show what a class cannot do. In *D.A. v. Noem* (D.D.C. 2025), the plaintiffs had already been flown to Ghana, and the court held it had no power to make another country keep them. In *Zapata v. Mullin* (D.D.C. 2026), a court ordered the return of a woman sent to the Congo on a narrower statutory theory: the Congo had refused to accept her. And individual cases are where the government has won. *Lopez v. ICE* (W.D. Tex. 2026) lifted a stay of removal to the Congo on the strength of diplomatic assurances, and *Bojorquez v. Baker* (D. Md. 2025) denied relief before the Fourth Circuit stepped in. A class judgment produces one answer. Individual habeas produces a spread of answers, and that spread is what a circuit split is made of.

The striped circuits on the map are where this happened. In the District of Maryland, *Cruz-Medina* barred a removal to Mexico until an immigration judge reviews the asylum officer's negative fear finding. In the Southern District of Texas, *Sagastizado Sanchez v. Noem* held that people facing third country removal have a right to meaningful notice and a chance to be heard. In New Jersey, a court barred the transfer of a woman arrested for removal to Ghana. *Ansumana S.* barred a removal to Sierra Leone even though that country had given diplomatic assurances, and *Arostegui-Maldonado v. Baltazar* (D. Colo. 2025) barred a removal while the court decided the case.

## How many habeas cases

The individual road is far busier than the class docket suggests. I counted it two ways.

**Cases.** [Habeas Dockets](https://habeasdockets.org), a volunteer project that collects documents from immigration habeas cases, tracks about 87,500 cases. A full text search of its documents returns **3,595 cases** in which the petition, the government's return, or an order mentions a "third country," and 1,879 that use the phrase "third country removal." That is a floor, not a census: the search covers only documents the project has collected, and its coverage varies by district.

**Pure and hybrid.** Of the 2,363 of those cases that the site's court filter assigns to a specific district, **1,707, or 72 percent, also cite *Zadvydas v. Davis***, the prolonged detention case. Those are hybrid petitions: the person holds protection from return to their home country, ICE has not found a country to take them, and the petition asks for release while also demanding notice and a hearing if a third country is found. The other 656 do not cite *Zadvydas*, and include the purer challenges to third country procedures.

**Decisions.** A Westlaw search for decisions since March 1, 2025 that pair a third country reference with notice or fear language returns **838**: 830 from district courts, 6 from courts of appeals, and 2 from the Supreme Court.

Both counts point the same way. The Ninth Circuit accounts for 59 percent of the district attributed Habeas Dockets cases and 65 percent of the Westlaw decisions, which fits Judge Bea's complaint that *Ibarra-Perez* opened district courts there to these claims. The Fifth and Eleventh Circuits follow, the Eleventh driven by the Middle District of Florida and the Middle District of Georgia, home of the Stewart Detention Center. The map's third view shows these counts by circuit.

For scale, ProPublica counts more than 85,000 immigration habeas petitions filed since January 2025. Third country claims are a small share of that flood, but they number in the thousands, against one court of appeals decision on the policy.

## The one government win

Not every district court has agreed. In *Lopez v. U.S. Immigration & Customs Enforcement*, 832 F. Supp. 3d 617 (W.D. Tex. 2026), a Bolivian woman with protection as to Bolivia, appearing without a lawyer, opposed her removal to the Congo. DHS had signed a third country removal agreement with the Congo on December 24, 2025 and found its diplomatic assurances credible on January 12, 2026. Judge Pulliam lifted the stay of removal, holding that those assurances, under the March 2025 guidance, gave her all the process she was due. He relied on *Munaf v. Geren* and on then Judge Kavanaugh's concurrence in *Kiyemba v. Obama*, which treat the Executive's judgment about a foreign government's assurances as one courts should not second guess.

Four months later, *Ansumana S.* reached the opposite result on similar facts, holding that assurances from Sierra Leone did not replace notice and a hearing. That disagreement goes to the heart of Question 3, because the guidance allows removal with no notice at all when a country has given assurances. The Fifth Circuit is shaded as split on the map.

## The Fourth Circuit appeal

One of those cases is now in a court of appeals. In *Bojorquez v. Baker*, the District of Maryland denied a preliminary injunction on November 7, 2025 in a challenge to a removal to Mexico after a negative USCIS fear screening. The petitioner appealed. The Fourth Circuit stayed his removal, ordered supplemental briefing, and on January 26, 2026 granted a stay of removal pending appeal, No. 25-6996. Briefing closed on April 29, 2026, and the docket I reviewed, current through May 24, shows no argument or decision. If the Fourth Circuit rules before the Supreme Court does, it will be the second court of appeals to speak to third country procedures, and the first to reach the merits of those procedures in an individual habeas case rather than a class action. That is the crosshatched circuit on the map.

## The split that does exist

Question 1 asks whether the district court had jurisdiction. The main fight is over 8 U.S.C. 1252(g), which bars any claim "arising from" a decision to "execute removal orders." In *Ibarra-Perez v. United States*, 154 F.4th 989 (9th Cir. 2025), a Cuban man with withholding as to Cuba was sent to Mexico about sixteen hours after he was told, over his repeated objection that he feared it. The Ninth Circuit held that 1252(g) did not bar his damages suit, because he challenged ICE's separate decision about where to send him and its legal authority to do so without process, not its discretion over whether or when to remove him.

On June 25, 2026, the Ninth Circuit denied rehearing en banc. Judge Bea, joined by eleven colleagues, wrote that the panel's reading "has been rejected by every other circuit that has considered it," and named four: the Third Circuit in *Tazu*, the Seventh in *E.F.L. v. Prim*, the Eighth in *Silva*, and the Eleventh in *Camarena*. Those cases did not involve third country removals, but they frame the jurisdiction question the Court will answer first. The count is less tidy than it looks. In *Ansumana S.*, a Minnesota district court heard a third country claim anyway, relying on the Eighth Circuit's older exception in *Jama v. INS*, 329 F.3d 630 (8th Cir. 2003), for habeas claims that present a pure question of law. Judge Bea also collected district courts across the Ninth Circuit, and some outside it, that have relied on *Ibarra-Perez* to hear third country claims. The Solicitor General has until October 23, 2026 to seek review of *Ibarra-Perez*.

## Why Question 2 matters beyond this case

Question 2 decides whether the class road stays open. If 1252(f)(1) bars classwide declaratory relief and vacatur, every challenge to the next immigration policy will look like the bond fight and like the thousands of third country petitions counted above: one detainee at a time, in the district where he is held, with a circuit split as the eventual result. If it does not, more of these questions will be settled in a single forum, and maps like this one will stay mostly empty. Either way, the Court is not only deciding this policy. It is choosing which of the two roads the next one travels.
